Instructor notes

How to deliver the Variant Calling module (by Arporn Wangwiwatsin)

The variant calling module is structured to move students from core fundamentals to complex interpretations, focusing on providing enough confidence for self-directed exploration rather than total mastery.

The key lessons and topics for teaching this module include:

1. Core Fundamentals and Jargon

  • Terminology: Familiarise students with a wide range of “jargons” and definitions, such as germline vs somatic mutations, ploidy, and specific variant types like SNV, SNP, and Indels.

  • File Formats: Introduce the VCF (Variant Call Format) file type, specifically VCF5, and teach how to work with these files.

  • Software Tools: The primary software used is BCFtools (with some mention of SAMtools for handling input from the read alignment module).

2. The Variant Calling Workflow

  • Step-by-Step Commands: Students should learn the progression of BCFtools commands: starting with mpileup, followed by call, then exploring and filtering the variants.

  • Filtering Techniques: A crucial lesson involves understanding the difference between annotating filters (marking variants as pass/fail) and hard filters using bcftools view.

  • Individual vs Joint Calling: Teach the practicalities of performing variant calling on individual samples versus joint calling on multiple samples simultaneously, including the benefits of the latter.

3. Biological and Technical Complexities

  • Biological Factors: Discuss how mutation rates, ploidy, and genomic regions (e.g., difficult-to-map areas) affect calling.

  • Technical Artifacts: Address how library preparation, PCR artifacts, and mapping errors can create noise in the data.

  • Mathematical Concepts: Introduce the underlying Bayesian thinking and probabilities used in statistical models to assess quality, though the module avoids going too deep into complex equations.

4. Assessment and Visualization

  • Reliability Criteria: Teach students how to assess whether called variants are reliable by looking at quality scores and specific criteria.

  • Visualization: Use IGV (Integrative Genomics Viewer) to visualize variants, which helps students distinguish between real variations and data noise.

  • Annotation (Bonus): Advanced sessions may cover how to annotate variants to assess their biological consequences.

5. Common Pitfalls and Misconceptions

  • The “Pass” Filter: Clarify that a “Pass” status only means a variant met specific user-defined criteria and does not guarantee it is real.

  • Reference Mismatches: Warn students about the common error of using mismatched versions of a reference genome during mapping and annotation.

  • Ambiguous Terms: Clarify terms that may have different meanings depending on the field, such as MAF, which can refer to “Minor Allele Frequency” or “Mutation Annotation Format”.

Teaching Strategies

  • Go Slow: Teachers are advised to be patient and prioritize understanding over speed.

  • Focus on Documentation: Rather than memorizing syntax (like specific command-line flags), teach students how to look through the manual as tools and syntax often change.

  • Active Recall: Use opportunities to ask students to explain terms (e.g., “What is an SNP?”) to reinforce learning.

  • Scenario-Based Learning: Pose questions about how different platforms (like Oxford Nanopore) or specific errors might affect data interpretation to encourage critical thinking.

Common hurdles encountered by course participants

1. Terminology and Jargon Overload

  • Vocabulary Burdens: One of the primary challenges is the sheer volume of new jargons and terms students must learn, such as germline vs. somatic mutations, ploidy, and various mutation types like SNV, SNP, and Indels.

  • Ambiguous Acronyms: Terms like MAF can be confusing because they have different meanings (Minor Allele Frequency vs. Mutation Annotation Format) depending on whether the student is coming from a general genomics or cancer genomics background.

  • Inconsistent Tool Language: Different software tools often use different terminologies to describe the same biological variants, adding to the confusion.

2. Technical and Command-Line Challenges

  • Counterintuitive Syntax: Students often find BCFtools commands counterintuitive; for example, using a query to explore a file, while “filters” are used for annotation rather than actual filtering.

  • Command Confusion: There is often confusion regarding bcftools view, which is used both to view BCF files and to perform “hard filters”.

  • Syntax Memorization: Students struggle with remembering specific command-line flags (e.g., -S, -I, or -U) rather than knowing how to navigate the manual for answers.

  • VCF Formatting: The structure and formatting of VCF files themselves can appear confusing to beginners.

3. Biological and Mathematical Complexity

  • Messy Data: Sequencing data is inherently messy due to biological complexities like varying mutation rates across the genome and different ploidy levels.

  • Technical Artifacts: Students must learn to distinguish real variants from technical noise, such as PCR artifacts, mapping errors, and sequencing quality issues.

  • Mathematical “Off-putting” Concepts: The underlying statistical models—specifically Bayesian thinking, probabilities, and complex equations—can be intimidating or “off-putting” for some learners.

  • Context-Specific Interpretation: Determining if a variant is “real” requires specific knowledge of the disease or organism, which can be overwhelming for students.

4. Visualization and Practical Implementation

  • IGV Configuration: In the structural variation module, a major hurdle is learning how to reconfigure IGV settings (e.g., grouping alignments by strand or squishing views) to properly visualize large-scale variants.

  • Theory vs. Data: Students often struggle to translate the theoretical schematics of variants into what they actually see in real sequencing data.

  • Infrastructure Issues: Technical difficulties, such as difficulties loading reference files into IGV when using cloud-based environments, can stall practical progress.

5. Common Misconceptions

  • The “Pass” Filter Myth: A common misconception is that any variant marked with a “Pass” filter is definitively real, when it actually only means the variant met the specific (and potentially low) criteria set by the user.

  • Reference Mismatches: A frequent error involves using mismatched versions of a reference genome between the mapping and annotation stages.
