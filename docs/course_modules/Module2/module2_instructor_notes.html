Instructor notes

1 How to deliver the Read Alignment module (by Carlo Lapid)

1.1 1. Situating the Module within a Broader Workflow

It is essential to emphasize that read alignment does not happen in a vacuum; it is the “first foray” into the actual analysis pipeline. Instructors should build on previous modules—such as Linux basics and Quality Control (QC)—and explicitly connect alignment to future steps like variant calling. Drawing these connections helps participants retain lessons better than teaching the module in isolation.

1.2 2. Instilling “Bioinformatic Thinking”

Beyond teaching technical skills, use this module to help students think like bioinformaticians. This involves:

  • Algorithmic Thinking: Briefly touching on how the computer “sees” the data through approaches like hash tables or the Smith-Waterman algorithm.

  • Resource Management: Discussing the computational costs (time, storage, and money) associated with handling large genomic datasets.

  • Pipeline Logic: Using Unix pipes in exercises to demonstrate how data “flows” from one tool to another.

1.3 3. Connecting to Bench Science Expertise

Since the audience often consists of bench scientists, instructors should link bioinformatics concepts to laboratory procedures they already understand. For example:

  • Relate PCR duplicates in the data to the PCR amplification step in library preparation.

  • Explain the necessity of merging files by discussing experimental designs, such as splitting samples across multiple sequencing lanes or runs.

1.4 4. Using Visualization as a Teaching Tool

The use of a genome browser like IGV is a critical pedagogical tool. It provides a “break” from the command line and allows participants to visualize the results of their work. Instructors should encourage students to “smell the flowers”—to simply explore the genome and see how deletions, insertions, or clipping manifest visually.

1.5 5. Managing Information Overload

Read alignment involves a high risk of information overload due to the complexity of file formats (SAM/BAM), CIGAR strings, and mapping qualities. Instructors should be patient and focus on the fundamentals. A sign of overload is when a student is happy just because a command ran without an error; the goal is to move them toward a deeper understanding of the output.

1.6 6. Encouraging Active Engagement with Commands

To prevent participants from mindlessly copy-pasting or typing commands without comprehension, instructors should ask probing questions. For instance, ask: “Why did you use the -M parameter? What does it do?”. Encourage them to examine inputs and outputs—such as looking at the reference genome or the SAM file—before moving to the next step.

1.7 7. Clarifying Technical Nuances

Certain technical aspects are frequent points of confusion and require explicit attention:

  • Sorting and Indexing: These are technically separate from alignment but are required for downstream analysis; students often forget their importance.

  • Qualities: Ensure students distinguish between read quality (from the sequencer) and mapping quality (from the aligner), even though both use the Phred scale.

  • Flags and CIGAR Strings: Use visualization to explain what specific SAM flags (e.g., 163 vs. 99) or CIGAR string notations actually mean in a biological context.

1.8 8. Redefining Success

The ultimate metric of success is not just finishing the exercise, but the “aha moment” when a student understands a concept, such as seeing a variant for the first time in a browser. Success is achieved when participants leave with a better appreciation of the bioinformatician’s perspective on data and workflows.

2 Common hurdles encountered by course participants

2.1 1. Information Overload

A significant hurdle is the risk of information overload because read alignment does not happen in a vacuum. To understand the process, students must simultaneously grasp:

  • Underlying read technology and laboratory workflows.

  • The theory of read mapping versus the practical execution of commands.

  • The complex structure of data files, including CIGAR strings and SAM flags.

  • Downstream connections, such as how alignment affects variant calling.

2.2 2. Surface-Level Understanding

A common sign of a struggling student is a focus on command execution rather than comprehension. Participants are often happy simply because a command ran without an error, without truly understanding what the program did or what the output signifies. This often leads to a reliance on copy-pasting commands rather than understanding what specific parameters actually do.

2.3 3. Technical and Format Confusion

Specific technical aspects of the data formats are frequent sticking points:

  • Quality Metrics: While both read quality and mapping quality use the Phred scale, students often confuse the two or struggle to distinguish their different meanings.

  • SAM Flags and CIGAR Strings: The binary-based SAM flags (e.g., why a pair is 163/83 versus 99/147) and the notation in CIGAR strings are difficult to interpret.

  • Visual Complexity: Even graphical tools like IGV can be intimidating because there is “so much to explore” and a large amount of information presented at once.

2.4 4. Procedural and Interpretational Omissions

  • Sorting and Indexing: Participants often forget or misunderstand sorting and indexing because these are technically separate steps from the alignment itself, yet they are essential for downstream analysis.

  • QC Interpretation: Students frequently struggle to interpret quality control results, asking for guidance on what constitutes “good” or “bad” data, such as whether a specific duplication rate is acceptable for their research.

  • Merging Logic: Understanding when it is appropriate to merge results from multiple alignments—and how this relates to experimental design or sequencing lanes—is a common conceptual challenge.

2.5 5. Algorithmic Complexity

Participants with little programming experience may find it difficult to think algorithmically. Concepts like hash tables, suffix trees, and the Smith-Waterman algorithm can be confusing, particularly regarding how scores are calculated or how gaps are identified in a sequence.
